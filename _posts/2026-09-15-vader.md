**VADER** (Visibility-Graph-Based Autonomous Decentralized Encounter-Triggered Replanning) lets a team of robots reach their own goals in an unknown environment: a building none of them has mapped. Each robot plans alone until it meets a teammate. The robots then merge what they have each seen into one shared map, decide who goes first through each passage, and split up again, keeping what they learned from each other.

First-author work at the SJTU Robotics Autonomy and Planning Lab, supervised by [Prof. Zhongqiang (Richard) Ren](https://wonderren.github.io/) and co-supervised by [Dr. Shenghai Yuan](https://scholar.google.com/citations?user=XcV_sesAAAAJ&hl=en). The paper, *VADER: Decentralized Multi-Robot Navigation in Unknown Environments via Encounter-Triggered Visibility Graph Merging* (Dun Yan Ong, Xuemian Wu, Shenghai Yuan, Zhongqiang Ren), is under review at one of the top IEEE robotics conferences.

## The problem: navigating unknown environments as a team

When robots head to their own goals in an unknown environment, they have to discover routes as they drive and also agree on who waits when two of them need the same corridor. They can only talk to teammates nearby. Reactive collision avoidance tends to deadlock in narrow passages, with both robots holding back until time runs out. Classic multi-agent path finding solves "who goes first", but it needs a map that nobody has yet.

## The idea

1. **Plan alone.** Each robot builds its own map and plans its own route as it explores.
2. **Merge on encounter.** When robots come within range, they combine what they have each seen into one shared map.
3. **Agree who goes first.** On that shared map, the group plans together, deciding routes and the order robots pass through narrow spaces.
4. **Keep what you learned.** When the robots move apart, each keeps the others' map, so it can use routes a teammate already explored.

## Results

We evaluated VADER in simulation across four kinds of environment (warehouse, indoor building, tunnels and a construction site) with teams of 2 to 10 robots. We compared it against reactive navigation baselines, and against an oracle planner that is given the true map in advance.

VADER brought every robot to its goal in **87%** of runs, against **53%** for the best reactive baseline. The gap widens with larger teams: **82% vs 29%** with six or more robots. That's close to the oracle's 95%, even though VADER starts with no map at all.

## What VADER builds on {#background}

- **Visibility graphs.** A visibility graph links obstacle corners that can see each other, and the shortest path between two points runs along it ([Lozano-Pérez & Wesley, 1979](https://doi.org/10.1145/359156.359164)). Classically it is built from a known map. In VADER each robot grows its own graph from what it has seen, and robots merge their graphs when they meet.
- **Conflict-Based Search (CBS).** A standard optimal solver for multi-agent path finding (MAPF): it plans each robot on its own, then resolves collisions between their plans ([Sharon et al., 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2014.11.006)). It assumes a known map and one central planner. VADER runs a CBS variant only among robots that have met, on a space-time version of their merged visibility graph. I built CBS from scratch before starting VADER: [Conflict-Based Search in C++](/posts/multi-agent-path-finding/).
- **The Robotarium.** Georgia Tech's remote-access testbed, where researchers upload code that then runs on real robots ([Wilson et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1109/MCS.2019.2949973)).
